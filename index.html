A few dropped packets can stall a water plant — no encryption broken.

Industrial plants are moving their SCADA control systems to the cloud — which puts the link between on-site equipment and the control room out on the public Internet. ICS-Sniper shows that an on-path attacker can disrupt these plants by reading only the shape of that encrypted traffic — packet sizes, timing, and direction — and dropping a small, precisely chosen set of packets.

  • No decryption
  • No network access
  • No knowledge of the control logic

Why this matters now

The control room is moving to the cloud

SCADA is the software that watches every sensor and coordinates every pump and valve in a plant. It used to live on-site, on a private network. Increasingly, water, wastewater, and manufacturing operators run it on cloud platforms instead — utilities across the US, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand are among the early adopters.

Lower cost

No SCADA hardware or local IT staff to run at every remote site.

Remote operations

Operators monitor and control geographically spread-out assets from anywhere.

Built-in resilience

Cloud providers offer uptime guarantees that on-site systems can't match cheaply.

The trade-off: the plant's most time-sensitive control traffic now crosses the open Internet. To protect it, operators follow the standard NIST and AWS playbook — authenticate the endpoints, add a firewall and intrusion detection, wrap the traffic in a VPN, and watch for high-volume denial-of-service. This is exactly the architecture ICS-Sniper targets, and it slips past all four safeguards.

The setup in the wild: PLCs on site, SCADA in the cloud, an encrypted VPN across the Internet — and an adversary who only needs a foothold on one router in between.

The short version

An on-path adversary can degrade a safety-critical plant by dropping under 5% of its packets — and stay invisible to today's detectors.

Who

A rogue insider at an ISP, or a nation-state with access to a router on the path — the same foothold behind recent Cisco and Juniper router vulnerabilities.

Where

Outside the plant perimeter, on the public Internet leg between the site and its cloud-hosted SCADA.

What

Watch the encrypted VPN flow, learn its rhythm, then drop the right packets at the right moment to desynchronize the process.

Why now

The exposure is new — it only exists once SCADA leaves the plant's private network. And the usual defenses (VPN, firewalls, DoS monitoring) give a false sense of safety against it.

How it works

Encryption hides the payload, not the pattern

PLCs talk to SCADA in a steady, repeating rhythm. Each state of the process has its own burst pattern, and that pattern shifts the moment the process is about to change state. VPN encryption scrambles the contents, but it can't hide when packets arrive, how big they are, or which way they're going. ICS-Sniper reconstructs the rhythm from that metadata alone.

encrypted VPN flow · adversary's view

The attacker never sees payloads — only the rhythm of the flow. Each PLC repeats its own burst pattern; together they form a repeating superperiod. Amber marks the two windows ICS-Sniper drops.

Stage 1 — profiling (offline)

Learn the plant's rhythm

ICS-Sniper records the encrypted flow over full operational cycles and recovers the repeating structure, then flags the windows where a state change is coming.

  • Recovers the superperiod using phase folding on packet timing and sizes
  • Ranks critical superperiods where the burst pattern shifts
  • Filters out retransmits and keep-alives — no ground truth needed
Stage 2 — attack (live)

Take the shot

On a later cycle, ICS-Sniper waits for the predicted moment and drops only the payload packets in the two targeted windows — including any retransmissions, so the update never lands.

  • Hits the superperiod before, and during, a state change
  • Drops ~20% of packets (unconnected) or 100% (connected)
  • Breaks the timed hand-off between sub-processes
Superperiod

When several PLCs share one tunnel, their individual rhythms combine into a single repeating pattern. That composite period — the least common multiple of the PLC periods — is the superperiod. Find it, and you can predict when the traffic will change.

Phase folding

A noise-resistant technique borrowed from astronomy for finding a hidden period in messy time-series data. ICS-Sniper applies it to overlap durations, inter-packet gaps, and packet sizes to lock onto the superperiod even under jitter and packet loss.

Critical superperiod

A window where the packet count or the set of packet sizes changes from the previous window — a strong signal that the process is about to switch state. These are the windows worth attacking.

Results

Tested on two water-treatment testbeds

The team built two validated testbeds based on the six-stage Secure Water Treatment (SWaT) plant, each with SCADA on a cloud VM over an OpenVPN tunnel — to their knowledge, the first cloud-hosted-SCADA-over-VPN testbeds. One uses simulated PLCs (S-SWaT); the other real Allen-Bradley PLCs (H-SWaT).

Process-delay attack

first 2 superperiods · ~4.7% of packets

Dropping payloads during the plant's start-up broke five state transitions at once, stalling the sub-processes as they tried to synchronize. Clean-water pumping started roughly 30 minutes late on the software testbed — and 2 minutes late on the hardware testbed, which then needed 3 extra minutes of runtime to hit its target.

Ultrafiltration-depletion attack

superperiods 31–32 · ~4.1% of packets

By delaying one "pump stopped" message between the backwash and ultrafiltration stages, the attack held a valve open 9 minutes and 11 seconds too long. In a real plant that means treated water draining away and a stalled filtration cycle.

Why it hides

Built-for-floods detectors don't see a whisper

An ensemble of eight state-of-the-art ICS detectors — network-based and process-log-based — was trained and run against the attack. In two of five scenarios no detector alerted during the attack at all; in the rest, alerts only arrived after the plant had already been degraded.

01

Network detectors wake on packets, not on silence

Their logic fires when a packet arrives. A quiet gap on the link — exactly what a dropped burst looks like — triggers nothing. That design is fine for high-rate floods and blind to a targeted blackhole.

02

Nothing conventional defenses look for

No spoofed ARP, no altered payloads, no extra traffic, no volume spike. Cloud DDoS and MITM defenses are tuned for the web services that dominate cloud traffic, not for a handful of missing OT packets.

03

At least 80% fewer packets than a volumetric attack

Because it only touches two short windows, ICS-Sniper drops far fewer packets than a naive blackhole aiming for the same effect — and it leaves liveness-check probes untouched, so the link still looks alive.

04

Detection would need a new approach

Catching this likely means actively polling the link in step with each PLC's rhythm — an unsolved research problem, not a setting on an existing tool.

What defenders can do

Where to push back

The attack needs a compromised on-path device to exist — so hardening routers and monitoring the ISP path still matters. Beyond that, the paper points to three directions, none of them fully solved yet.

Shape the traffic

Pad and re-time packets so the periodic burst pattern no longer leaks when a state change is coming. The catch: PLC timing is tuned for control performance, so hiding the rhythm without hurting the process is hard.open challenge

Diversify the path

Route OT traffic over multiple, path-diverse Internet links (for example with SCION-style source routing) so a single compromised hop can't blackhole the flow.

Poll for liveness, on rhythm

Actively probe the site-to-SCADA link in sync with each PLC's period so a silent gap becomes visible — a promising but non-trivial detection direction.open challenge

Code & artifacts

Reproduce it yourself

Everything is open source across five repositories: the attack, the detector ensemble, and three testbeds. Preprocessed traces and SCADA logs ship with the repos, so you can reproduce the profiling and detection results with no live testbed, no special hardware, and no GPU.

The attack

github.com/ICS-Sniper/ICS-SNIPER-attack

The profiling module (phase folding over traffic metadata to find superperiods and rank critical windows) plus the packet-dropping controller. Needs Python 3.10 and tshark.

Detector ensemble

github.com/ICS-Sniper/SOTA-detectors-NDSS

The IPAL ensemble used to measure stealth — Kitsune, inter-arrival-time, PASAD, InvariantRules and more — with detection-lag and alert-overlap scoring.

H-SWaT — hardware testbed

github.com/ICS-Sniper/Modbus-C-hardware-testbed

Real Allen-Bradley Micro800 PLCs running connected Modbus/TCP over OpenVPN — the realistic-timing testbed.

S-SWaT — Modbus software testbed

github.com/ICS-Sniper/Modbus-U-software-testbed

Software-simulated PLCs running unconnected Modbus/TCP over OpenVPN for fast prototyping.

S-SWaT — ENIP software testbed

github.com/ICS-Sniper/ENIP-U-software-testbed

Software-simulated PLCs running unconnected ENIP/TCP over OpenVPN.

ICS-Sniper: A Targeted Blackhole Attack on Encrypted ICS Traffic
Research paper #2779, submitted to the Network and Distributed System Security (NDSS) Symposium. Authors anonymized for peer review.

Responsible disclosure: all attacks were run only on emulated and cloud-based testbeds with no physical components. The reference SWaT plant's real OT system is not connected to the Internet, so this work cannot be used against it. Published to help operators and researchers build stronger defenses for cloud-connected OT networks — not to enable attacks.
